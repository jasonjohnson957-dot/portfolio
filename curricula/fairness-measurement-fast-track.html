DAY 01Where bias actually enters

The Bias Taxonomy Across the ML Lifecycle

Before you measure fairness in a model's outputs, you need to know that "bias" isn't one thing. It enters at different points in the lifecycle, and each entry point needs a different fix — no metric at the output end repairs a problem introduced upstream. This is the mental model auditors use to scope an assessment.

StageBias typeWhat it looks like
Before data existsHistorical biasThe world itself was unequal — e.g. past promotion data reflects a workplace that historically favored one group, so even a "perfectly accurate" model learns to reproduce that pattern.
Data collectionRepresentation biasThe training sample under- or over-represents parts of the deployment population (rare disease data skewed toward one demographic, for instance).
Data collectionMeasurement biasFeatures or labels are noisier, or mean something different, across groups — e.g. "prior arrests" as a proxy for "criminal behavior" when policing intensity differs by neighborhood.
ModelingAggregation biasOne model is fit to a population where the true feature–outcome relationship actually differs by subgroup, so the "average" model underperforms for everyone at the edges.
EvaluationEvaluation biasThe benchmark or test set used to validate the model doesn't reflect who it will actually be used on.
DeploymentDeployment biasThe model is used in a context or workflow different from what it was validated for — a triage-support tool becomes an autonomous gatekeeper.
Why this matters for your job Every fairness metric you'll learn this week measures a symptom at the output stage. Your value as a governance practitioner is tracing an unfair outcome back to which of these six points it actually came from — because the remediation is completely different for each one (better data collection vs. a different model vs. a policy change vs. a deployment constraint).
DAY 02The shared vocabulary

Confusion Matrix Refresher — Tied to Fairness

Every group fairness metric is a comparison of confusion-matrix quantities across two or more groups. If you're solid on this, the rest of the week is just "which of these ratios do we require to be equal."

Core rates TPR (Recall / Sensitivity) = TP / (TP + FN) — "of those who truly deserve a positive outcome, how many got one" FPR = FP / (FP + TN) — "of those who truly don't, how many got one anyway" PPV (Precision) = TP / (TP + FP) — "of those who got a positive outcome, how many actually deserved it" Selection Rate = (TP + FP) / N — "what fraction of this group got a positive outcome, period" Base Rate (Prevalence) = (TP + FN) / N — "what fraction of this group truly belongs to the positive class"

We'll use one running example for the rest of the week: CareBridge AI's sepsis-alert model, which flags ED patients for expedited clinician review (positive = flagged). It's evaluated across two patient groups, A and B, drawn from the same hospital system.

DAY 03Equal outcomes

Demographic Parity & Disparate Impact

Demographic Parity (also called Statistical Parity) requires the same fraction of each group to receive the positive outcome, regardless of whether they actually belong to the positive class.

Definition P(Ŷ=1 | A=a) = P(Ŷ=1 | A=b) for every group a, b

In practice you'll rarely see exact equality — instead you'll see the Disparate Impact Ratio, borrowed from U.S. employment law (the EEOC's "four-fifths rule," 1978): the selection rate of the lower-selected group divided by the selection rate of the higher-selected group. A ratio below 0.80 is the traditional flag for adverse impact.

Worked example (CareBridge) Selection rate, Group A = (250+50)/500 = 0.60 Selection rate, Group B = (150+50)/500 = 0.40 Disparate Impact Ratio = 0.40 / 0.60 = 0.667 → fails the four-fifths rule
When this metric is the right call Contexts where exposure/access itself is the harm regardless of underlying qualification — ad targeting, loan pre-approval invitations, who gets shown a job posting at all.
Its blind spot Demographic parity says nothing about whether the groups actually differ in true positive rate. Forcing equal selection rates when base rates genuinely differ (Group A's 56% vs. Group B's 50%, from Day 2) can mean rejecting qualified people in one group or flagging unqualified people in the other — "leveling down" rather than fixing anything. This is exactly the critique to raise when someone hands you a disparate-impact-only audit and calls it done.
DAY 04Equal error rates

Equal Opportunity & Equalized Odds

These two, from Hardt, Price & Srebro (2016), fix demographic parity's blind spot by conditioning on the true outcome instead of ignoring it.

Equal Opportunity P(Ŷ=1 | Y=1, A=a) = P(Ŷ=1 | Y=1, A=b) → equal True Positive Rate across groups. "Everyone who truly qualifies has an equal chance of being correctly flagged."
Equalized Odds Equal Opportunity, PLUS: P(Ŷ=1 | Y=0, A=a) = P(Ŷ=1 | Y=0, A=b) → equal True Positive Rate AND equal False Positive Rate across groups.
Worked example (CareBridge) TPR, Group A = 250/280 = 0.893 TPR, Group B = 150/250 = 0.600 → Equal Opportunity gap = 0.293 (fails badly) FPR, Group A = 50/220 = 0.227 FPR, Group B = 50/250 = 0.200 → FPR gap = 0.027 (roughly holds)

So this model actually clears the FPR bar but badly fails the TPR bar — Group B's truly-septic patients are far less likely to get flagged than Group A's. Demographic parity alone would have missed exactly what matters clinically here.

When this metric is the right call Contexts where a missed true positive is the dominant harm — medical alerting, fraud detection, safety-critical flags. This is the framing at the center of the ProPublica/COMPAS recidivism debate: which error type (false positive vs. false negative) is worse, and for whom, is a policy judgment the metric can't make for you.
DAY 05The uncomfortable math

Predictive Parity, Calibration & the Impossibility Theorem

Predictive Parity requires that a positive prediction mean the same thing across groups — same precision. Calibration is the finer-grained version for risk scores: among people given a risk score of, say, 0.7, the true positive rate should be 70% in every group.

Predictive Parity P(Y=1 | Ŷ=1, A=a) = P(Y=1 | Ŷ=1, A=b) Worked example: PPV, Group A = 250/300 = 0.833 PPV, Group B = 150/200 = 0.750 → gap = 0.083 (moderate)
The result you must be able to explain in an interview Chouldechova (2017) and Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan (2016) independently proved: if base rates differ across groups, a model cannot simultaneously satisfy Equalized Odds and Calibration — except in the trivial case of a perfect predictor. Our CareBridge example has different base rates (56% vs. 50%), so this isn't an edge case — it's the norm.

This is the single most important idea in the whole field, and it's why "just make the model fair" is not a well-formed instruction. You cannot optimize for every fairness definition at once. Someone — ideally documented, deliberate, and signed off, not defaulted — has to decide which error profile matters most for this specific use case. That decision, and its written rationale, is the actual audit artifact a governance practitioner produces.

Practice Write two sentences: for the CareBridge sepsis alert, which is worse — a missed true sepsis case (FN) or an unnecessary clinician review (FP) — and which fairness metric from Days 3–5 follows from that answer?
DAY 06Beyond group averages

Individual Fairness & Counterfactual Fairness

Everything above operates on group averages, which can mask harm to individuals or to intersectional subgroups (e.g., Group B women vs. Group B men). These two frameworks approach fairness person-by-person instead.

Individual Fairness (Dwork et al., 2012) "Similar individuals should receive similar outcomes" — formalized as a Lipschitz condition: distance(outcome_i, outcome_j) ≤ L · distance(individual_i, individual_j)

The catch: you need a defensible similarity metric — a way to say how similar two people are — and choosing that metric is itself a value judgment. This is rarely implemented in production; know it for the vocabulary, and to recognize when a vendor claims it without defining their distance function.

Counterfactual Fairness (Kusner et al., 2017) A decision is counterfactually fair if it would be the same in a hypothetical world where only the protected attribute changed, holding fixed everything causally upstream of it.

This requires a causal model of how the protected attribute influences other features — genuinely hard to specify correctly, but this is the concept regulators reach for when they ask "would this decision differ for an otherwise-identical applicant of a different group?" It's showing up in EU AI Act high-risk conformity conversations, so know the term even if you never build one yourself.

Practical takeaway Group metrics (Days 3–5) are what you'll compute 90% of the time. Individual and counterfactual fairness are what you cite when someone asks "but what about a specific person who was harmed, not just the group average?" — a question that comes up in every real audit.
DAY 07Tools + the deliverable

Tooling, and Writing the Fairness Audit Report

You don't need to be the one writing mitigation code, but you need to know what these tools do so you can read their output and ask the right follow-up question.

Fairlearn

Microsoft, Python. Metric dashboard plus mitigation algorithms (reduction methods, threshold optimization). Plugs into scikit-learn pipelines.

AIF360

IBM. The broadest metric library (70+), with pre-, in-, and post-processing bias mitigation algorithms. Python and R.

Aequitas

University of Chicago. Audit-first design — generates a bias report across many groups and metrics at once. Built for practitioners, not just ML engineers.

What-If Tool

Google PAIR. No-code, visual slicing and counterfactual exploration of a trained model — good for stakeholder demos and workshops.

The Fairness Evaluation Report — Structure

  1. Model context — what it does, who it affects, what a positive prediction triggers
  2. Protected attributes & proxies considered — including features that correlate with a protected attribute even if the attribute itself was excluded
  3. Metrics selected, and why — tie the choice explicitly to the harm profile (Day 5's exercise)
  4. Subgroup results — including intersectional slices, not just single-attribute groups
  5. Disparity findings & root-cause hypothesis — which lifecycle stage from Day 1 likely produced it
  6. Recommendation — mitigate, or accept the risk with documented rationale and sign-off
  7. Monitoring plan — what gets re-measured post-deployment, and at what cadence
Capstone Exercise Take a new contingency table (build one yourself, or reuse CareBridge with different numbers), compute all four metrics from this week, and write the one-paragraph "metrics selected, and why" section for a report. That paragraph is the actual skill — anyone can run the numbers; the judgment call is the job.

Glossary

Base rate / Prevalence
The true fraction of a group that actually belongs to the positive class.
Selection rate
The fraction of a group that received a positive prediction, regardless of ground truth.
Disparate impact ratio
Lower group's selection rate ÷ higher group's selection rate; below 0.80 traditionally flags adverse impact.
Demographic parity
Equal selection rates across groups.
Equal opportunity
Equal true positive rates across groups.
Equalized odds
Equal true positive rates AND equal false positive rates across groups.
Predictive parity
Equal precision (PPV) across groups.
Calibration
A risk score means the same thing across groups — a 0.7 score corresponds to a 70% true positive rate everywhere.
Impossibility theorem
Equalized odds and calibration cannot both hold when base rates differ across groups (except for a perfect predictor).
Individual fairness
Similar individuals should receive similar outcomes, formalized via a distance/similarity metric.
Counterfactual fairness
A decision is fair if it wouldn't change in a causal counterfactual world where only the protected attribute differed.
Proxy variable
A feature that correlates with a protected attribute closely enough to reintroduce its effect even when the attribute itself is excluded.
Intersectionality
Disparities that only appear when slicing by combinations of attributes (e.g., group × sex) rather than one attribute at a time.

Reading List (Priority Order)

  1. Barocas, Hardt & Narayanan — Fairness and Machine Learning. The canonical free textbook (fairmlbook.org). Read Ch. 1–3 first; it's the source most of this week is distilled from.
  2. Hardt, Price & Srebro (2016) — the paper that introduced Equal Opportunity and Equalized Odds.
  3. Chouldechova (2017) — the disparate-impact / calibration impossibility result, worked through a recidivism-scoring case.
  4. ProPublica's COMPAS investigation — read this for the real-world stakes behind Day 4 and Day 5; it's the case study everyone in this field references.
  5. Dwork, Hardt, Pitassi, Reingold & Zemel (2012) — Fairness Through Awareness, the individual fairness paper.
  6. Kusner, Loftus, Russell & Silva (2017) — Counterfactual Fairness.
  7. Suresh & Guttag — the lifecycle bias-sourcing framework behind Day 1's taxonomy.
  8. Mehrabi et al. (2021) — a broad survey if you want a single paper mapping the whole field's definitions.
